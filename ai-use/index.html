Percentage of papers with disclosed and apparent AI use

Every preprint whose first version was submitted in the month and whose LaTeX source could be read, as a percentage of those papers. Across the series 10 of the 3,660 submissions had no readable source (at most 2 in a month); they are left out of every count and percentage on this page.

Disclose AI useDisclose AI writingApparent AI writing

Disclose AI use counts papers that say anywhere in their text that an AI tool was used, for anything at all: code, computation, proof search, literature, language.
Disclose AI writing counts the papers whose statement says a model generated or helped write the text, in full or in part (the AI, AI* and AI+Human types below); proofreading alone does not count.
Apparent AI writing counts papers whose text carries the pattern that machine-drafted writing leaves, whether or not they say so.

Every paper gets a score from 0 to 18 based on selected markers of AI-style writing. A score of 5 or higher is classified as apparent AI writing. Method

The corpus is every arXiv preprint with primary category math.NT whose first version was submitted in the month. Nothing is sampled and nothing is left out. Each paper is read from the LaTeX source of its first version; later versions are not counted.

Disclosure is counted by searching that source for any mention of AI tools and reading every sentence found. Apparent AI writing is a score built from a fixed set of markers, primarily based on writing style and vocabulary use. The indicators are a combination of ideas from AI detection literature, my observations, and Claude's experiments. As a control, the same scoring was run on five months of arXiv number theory from well before large language models: 0.2% of those papers passed the threshold (2 of 870). The error in the other direction is much larger: among the papers whose authors say a model generated or helped write the text, only 54% (52 of 96) reach the threshold, and the other 46% are missed. The apparent AI writing counts are therefore likely a significant undercount.

I am not publishing the precise scoring method because:

  • I do not claim that it is a very good or accurate method, but I believe it is a decent rough approximation of flagging papers whose texts were likely generated in large part by AI
  • I do not claim that the method will scale well to other disciplines, or into the future
  • I do not want it to become a benchmark

Feel free to contact me if you would like to discuss the method in more detail.

Disclaimers

A score is not a verdict. It counts markers that generative AI tends to produce. It is not evidence that a model wrote a paper, and it says nothing about whether the mathematics is correct, original or worth reading. A paper written entirely by a person can score high, and a paper written largely by a model can score low.

Disclosure categories are interpreted based on text. Nothing an author states has been verified, or attempted to be verified. Authors write disclosures in different ways, some more vague than others, and judgement calls were largely automated. For instance, a paper written by a person from a model’s draft and a lightly edited model draft may be described identically, and some papers whose authors state the text is theirs carry the same markers as papers that disclose drafting. That may mean the prose was written by a person working from a model’s write-up, or that there is more model writing than the statement suggests. Disclosure categories, and most disclosure statements themselves, do not distinguish these.

Using AI is not misconduct. Nothing here is intended as an accusation, and I did not personally check or read most papers with high scores. The point of the study is the shape of the trend and the gap between use and disclosure, not any individual paper. That said, I personally believe authors should try to accurately and clearly disclose their use of AI at this time.

One subject. This is number theory on arXiv. Other fields and other repositories may look nothing like it.
